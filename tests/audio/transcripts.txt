Host: Welcome back to Learning Matters. My guest today is Doctor Helen Carter, who researches digital education. Helen, schools have invested heavily in technology over the past decade. Has it actually improved learning?
Dr Helen Carter: That's the question everyone wants a simple answer to, and I'm afraid there isn't one. Technology on its own doesn't improve or damage learning. What makes the difference is how a tool is used: by whom, for what purpose, and with what kind of support. A tablet can be a powerful way to practise pronunciation, or it can simply be a very expensive notebook.
Host: So you wouldn't argue that digital tools should replace traditional teaching?
Dr Helen Carter: Not at all. Nor would I suggest banning them. Teachers remain central. Good tools extend what a skilled teacher can do, rather than substituting for it.
Host: What concerns you most at the moment?
Dr Helen Carter: Access. We tend to assume that every student has a fast, reliable internet connection and a decent device at home. In reality, many don't. When homework depends on technology that some families simply can't rely on, we risk widening the very gaps we hoped to close.
Host: So what would you recommend to schools?
Dr Helen Carter: Start with the learning goal, not the product. Before adopting any tool, ask what students should be able to do afterwards, and then evaluate honestly whether the tool helps them get there. If it doesn't, it doesn't matter how impressive it looks, or how many other schools are using it.
Host: Doctor Helen Carter, thank you very much.
